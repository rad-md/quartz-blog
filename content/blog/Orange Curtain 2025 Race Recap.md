#### The Prelude

A few months back, a friend of mine (for the sake of this story, we'll call him "Ryan") told me he signed up for this 24 hour race that goes back and forth on a creek trail. My initial reaction when I heard this was that it sounded hilarious. 24 hours going back and forth on flat road? I couldn't imagine that being that hard, but it did sound really silly. Even reading this back, I can't believe thinking this race would be easy was something that crossed my mind. Why I thought this wouldn't be that hard was purely ego-driven and it was punished exorbitantly.

Among all of the different running flavors like sprinters, trail runners, and road runners, I lean heavily towards road historically. I live about a mile away from the entrance to a road creek trail in Irvine that spans all across the city, spanning around 20 miles (40 miles if I wanted to go all the way out and back). I've logged around 2000 miles going up and down this creek trail for the last two years. 

Over time, this 24 hour race slowly piqued my interest as one of my for-fun one-off ultras (previous ones I did were the [Taco Bell 50k](https://tacobell50k.com/) and the [Will Rogers 24h relay](https://www.khraces.com/series/will-rogers)). Some time in April, I pulled the trigger and spent the $250 to participate in this run: the Orange Curtain 24 hour.

#### The Preparation

Generally, I'm a detail-oriented and meticulous planner of scheduling my runs -- much to the chagrin of those that I run with. For example, for the 2026 Orange County Marathon (where I broke 4 hours for the first time), I didn't miss a single run in Pfitz's 12/60 program: a modification I made to his 12-week, 70 mpw peak program to make it more sustainable with my level of fitness and time. The same can be said about the 2025 Honolulu Marathon where I didn't miss a single workout in 12/55.

For Orange Curtain, I followed no plan and had no ideas about what would better prepare me for the run. For the two months leading up to the race, I averaged around 35 miles a week, down from my ~55 that I averaged going into the Orange County marathon at the start of May. My rationale is that I was taking a break from planned, intentional running and wanted to be more freeform for a bit before dedicating the second half of the year to upping my mileage and preparing for a race early next year. I can't say for certain if this was a mistake or not, since I'm not sure if the extra month of high quality ~50-60 mile weeks would have made a significant impact on my experience.

I didn't do anything like this for Orange Curtain. At this race, I knew I would be seeing solid foods at the aid stations every 5 kilometers so I went in with no internal guidance for taking gels. The bill came out to around $110. Over the course of my run, of these things, I only took 2 caffeine gummies ($4).

#### Race Day and Preparation

I generally wake up around 5-6AM and the race would start at 7PM. If I was going to last the full 24 hours, that meant I will have been awake for over 36. I ate the same breakfast (morning oats and pour-over coffee) that I eat 90% of days to give myself the best chance of having a successful race.

Ryan's mom was gracious enough to bring us to the race in Cerritos (around a 25 minute drive). After finding parking and getting to the start line, we got our bibs and chatted with a few of the other racers, of which there were only 16 of us total. We left our drop bags by a fence on the ground, which later would turn out to be a huge mistake. 

Yen, the race director, asked for a show of hands to see how many racers were doing this for the first time. It was only me and Ryan. Interestingly, Ryan and I were by far the youngest people at the race at 27 and 26, respectively. The average age of a racer was about 53, with the oldest participant being 70 (spoiler, but the 70 year old ran the same amount of out-and-backs as I did).

For my first lap, I didn't bring anything but my phone and some water. We lined up at the start line, where at 7pm sharp we set off at a blistering 12 min/mile pace.

#### The First Quarter

The start of the race I was in my comfort zone. It was peaceful at sunset. The air was cool and the sun was warm. On one side was Liberty Park, where droves of people were LTAO (laughing their ass off) and playing pickleball and hanging out on the grass. On the other side was the creek which had a familiar scent of sewage and pee. The vibes were so good that I was recording videos every mile with my pace like many running influencers do. 

Going 12 minute pace for an hour or two is something I've done nearly every day for two years -- most likely to my detriment as a runner trying to get faster and stronger. Ryan and I were slowly drifting paces, where one of us was maybe running ~15 seconds per mile faster than the other. After the first 10k out-and-back (ONB) finished, I grabbed some caffeine chews and my earphones since it was starting to get dark and I knew I was going to hit some long, solitary stretches of running.

Once night came and I had finished ~30 kilometers, I started to get really bored. I was no longer enthused by my podcasts and music. The race was so sparse that I'd be lucky to share just a few miles with a passerby. Around 10:30PM, almost four hours into the race, I called my friend Sarah who saved my mental for nearly 2 hours. It's a running joke that 10:30PM is long past my bedtime, but I really was feeling so sleepy by this point already that a conversation talking about our favorite animals prevented me from taking a break at the start line for a few hours and a nap. But once my call with Sarah ended around 12:15AM, just past the 5 hour mark, I was on my own again -- this time for the rest of the night.

#### The Grueling Middle

I shared a few miles with a guy named Jensen around mile 26-28. He said he ran his first 50k 3 weeks prior, and this was his first 100k race (incredible). We talked about family, life in Arizona, and ultrarunning as a sport. It was inspiring to me the support system that he had at this race: multiple people from his family stayed at the start line through the entire night and helped him out whenever he needed. Comparatively, I would return to a small plastic bag on the ground further off course that would eventually get drenched by sprinklers and caked in mud, making the respite of finishing another lap an unpleasant experience. I eventually slowed down and let him drop me because I had no business running 11 minute miles.

Miles 30-42 were by far the most difficult. There was no one to call since it was the dead of night. There were no sights to see. The morale had palpably shrunk - the chants of *Keep it up! You're doing great!* from those that I passed on the trail turned into soft grunts of encouragement. Everyone was handling this struggle to the best of their ability, hoping to get a second wind by the time the sun came up again.

These are the miles where it would have been great to have some people at the race, but I understand the sacrifice and love it takes for people to support you like this. It is not a fun experience being part of a crew: your loved one hobbles in. They might look terrible or have bloodshot eyes. They might be in a terrible mood, in a state where they just need their bottles filled and some more gels and some time alone before heading off into the dark again. The crew has to be locked in on supporting the runner while also being sleep-deprived themselves. It really does take a village.

At 3:59AM I texted Sofia.

![[cry_for_help.jpg]]

By the time I sent this text, I was already pretty deep in the pain cave. It was isolating. In previous races, running faster meant the end came sooner. Conversely, the goal here was to be moving for 24 straight hours, so no matter what I did the day moved at its own pace. 

Since the race was on a straight creek trail, I would be able to see the warm lights of the start line from nearly a mile away, but my feet hurt so bad that it would take me 15 minutes to get there by alternating running and walking. I was exhausted each time I reached that timing mat. Yet again and again, I ate a few chips, a few grapes, and set back out.

#### The End

Around mile 42, the sun started to rise again. The pickleballers started coming back. I felt my legs start to move a little better - during the previous 10 miles, I was averaging around a 16 min / mile pace, but I was back to 13-14 min miles when the sun came. I think the morale of the many was getting better too as I heard people's voices become a little more joyful. This was short-lived as the realization set in that I was still less than halfway through the 24 hours. 

When I reached the timing mat indicating that I completed 70 kilometers, I think I started hallucinating. A volunteer asked: "do you want to eat some breakfast, Rad?" I didn't tell anyone my name, and I signed up under the name Rafael. For a moment I wasn't sure what was real<sup>1</sup>. I started my next lap.

At 6:57AM, just about 12 hours into the race, Sofia was able to return my call. She asked if I was OK and I told her with shaky breath that I wasn't. She asked when I wanted to be picked up, and I said 8AM. She was coming.

A few minutes later, I ran into Ryan again on the trail. He had done a full outfit change and was running pretty strongly with a few other people who I think were doing the 100K. He drifted off from them for a moment for us to have a quick check-in with me, a consistent part of our run for the last 12 hours. I told him this was my last ONB and Sofia was on her way. He was shocked and said I could keep going, that I was running so well. Although I tried really hard not to, the tears finally burst out and I cried that it was just so hard. He understood the feeling better than anyone else and told me to wait for him at the start line. We parted ways for the final time this race.

On this final 5K running back to the start, I saw so many more people with cheerful demeanors. Other divisions of the race like the marathon, 6 hour, and 12 hour were starting around this time, so people with fresh legs zoomed past me (at probably around a 10-11 minute pace, while I was going 14). My watch beeped and I hit 50 miles for the first time. I imagined my first 50 to feel much more rewarding.

I reached the timing mat for the last time, finishing ~50.5 miles in around 12.5 hours. It took everything to stay standing and eat the various breakfast items they had. I had four slices of banana bread, some coke, and a few spam musubis -- a meal for champions. On my last lap, I saw a guy holding his shoes in his hand who I crossed paths with countless times. He was much faster than me for a lot of the race. I asked how he was doing, and he told me he had to drop out because of injury. He had to walk his entire last lap. I stopped myself from crying for him too.

Sofia came shortly after. Even though it was only one night, it felt like forever since I saw someone I loved.

We waited for Ryan for another 10 minutes to finish his lap. I think he was sad when he saw I was with Sofia, indicating I had finalized my decision to leave. He just finished a lap that put him around ~56 miles in. He had a lot left in him, I knew. After a brief moment of trying to convince me to just take a break and to keep going later, he resigned. We hugged and we cried, congratulating each other for the effort so far. Having gone through the same 12 hours he did, I could only imagine what kind of pain he would feel for the next 12. He set off on the trail. I set off home<sup>2</sup>.

The entire time I was on the trail, I wanted the time to go by faster. I wanted it to end. I wasn't having any fun. But when I was in the car, I was thinking I could have kept going. I told Sofia I would probably do this again.

#### Looking Forward

A few months prior to running this race, I read a post by race director [Lazarus Lake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lazarus_Lake). If you're interested in this post, it's the top comment of this [thread](https://www.reddit.com/r/ultrarunning/comments/pb9bfa/24_hr_track_strategy/). I wish I re-read this the day before or even the day of. Reading it now, the only thing that I gathered was that I should have a max pace of 12 min / mile, which I kept up pretty well. There are troves of other important information here that I think would have been super helpful. From just this post that I think would be pretty valuable:
1. "have a place to sleep for 5-10 minutes." This is not something I had at all. Every area of the ground near the start line was kind of damp and I only brought the supplies I mentioned above. If I were to do this in the future, I think I'd bring a camping chair or picnic blanket to just lay on the ground for a bit.
2. "start with a limit for a maximum forced walk. never walk more than 100 yards in an unplanned walk break." There were stretches where I walked for at least 10 minutes at a snail's pace.
3. "your 24 hour will have 3 parts. i feel great and i can run forever! this sucks and i will never make it to the end! i cant believe i can run again! only the third one is right." I got to the second part, and maaaybe felt a tinge of the third one when it was bright again. But i dwelled too much on the second one.
4. "a 24 hour does not truly begin for 12 hours". This one is pretty crazy, since I was feeling it pretty hard around 8 hours in. But probably what this means is that you can really gain ground on people that were ahead of you in the first 12 hours as long as you keep moving.

Another thing I noticed is that *no one* was crying at this race. Of course, a lot of people had been doing this race for years. I'll say something that makes me sound like a big asshole, because it's kind of a big asshole opinion (that I am reconsidering). At other races, particularly marathons, I didn't understand why people cried at the end, at least around where I was finishing around the 4 hour mark. For me, this felt like a pretty unremarkable time, so I didn't understand. But after crying halfway through a 24 hour race and seeing people just keep moving forward stone-faced, I understand that there are levels to this. My breaking point is around 12 hours. For others it's around 4. For others, maybe it's 24, 48, 72 hours. Maybe it's based on the effort they give on that day, or the culmination of the work they put in leading up to the race, or how vulnerable running for so long makes you feel. Or so many other things. I feel a deeper empathy for people that give it everything they have to finish a marathon, and this is probably an indication that in my marathons that's not what I'm doing.

I also think that my cardio wasn't the limiting factor in this race at all, but my leg muscles and joints felt super weak. I am a huge perpetrator of skipping leg day, but if I want to try again with good effort I need to commit to doing legs twice a week. I've saved countless videos on leg workouts for runners, but I think a holistic routine that hits the entire kinetic chain would give me the most bang for my buck. Similarly, I found that my biceps started really hurting around 8 hours into the race. While I thought I was running really easily and not engaging them too much for arm swing, I guess I was still doing it too much. I didn't see any tips online for this issue, so might just need more practice in longer runs.

I think I handled my food intake pretty well this race too. I wish I kept track of it a little better, but I basically just ate whatever I was craving at each aid station, which was some combination of watermelon, grapes, chips, and coke. For now, I think I'll just keep doing that for future runs of listening to my body of what it thinks it needs to keep going.

To handle the mental part of these races, I think I just have to do them more often. In the next race I do, I will try to internalize the feeling that I'll get my legs back eventually. I regret stopping before having the opportunity to feel that.

A joke that I had with Ryan during the race is that there was a "loved ones diff." This meant that we were weaker than others because we had no loved ones at this race. While I'd love to say that it shouldn't matter and that it's all mental and we can do it alone, I think having loved ones makes these races so much more tolerable. Just a few minutes of seeing people I love might help keep me going for more loops. 

What's next? For the first time since I started running, I don't have a single race on the calendar planned. Besides maybe the Turkey Trot, I think I won't sign up for anything else for the rest of the year. Currently, I am most interested in doing a 50-miler. 

I want to dedicate a long block to just improving my foundation. My previous peak weeks in training blocks is 60 miles, but I'd like to get to 70 and maybe hold that as my average for a few months. I'd like to commit to the gym work required to stay out on these longer runs, or even be able to put more effort in on shorter races like the marathon. Regardless of whatever's in the future, I'll just keep stacking miles.
